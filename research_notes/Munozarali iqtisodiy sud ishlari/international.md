# Landmark international / English-language B2B commercial contract cases for an Uzbek economic-court simulator

Method note: primary judgments were read in full text from The National Archives "Find Case Law" (caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk), which republishes UKSC/EWCA/EWHC judgments. BAILII and HSF returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches. The UKSC press summary (Triple Point) and the CISG-online translation (New Zealand mussels) were read as PDFs. The Uzbek Civil Code articles were checked against the official text on lex.uz. Where a figure comes only from a secondary source, it is marked **[secondary]**. Where something could not be verified, it is marked **[UNVERIFIED]**.

Corrections to the brief, all verified:
- *Classic Maritime v Limbungan* [2019] EWCA Civ 1102 is **not a COVID-19 case**. It concerns the Fundão (Samarco) dam burst in Brazil on 5 November 2015. It is still an excellent force majeure / "but-for" case.
- *ParkingEye v Beavis* (decided together with *Cavendish*) is a **consumer** case (a motorist), so it falls outside the B2B constraint. Use it only as a contrast. *Cavendish v Makdessi* is the B2B half.
- *Bates v Post Office* [2019] EWHC 606 (QB) was **not verified** in this research (see Gaps). Its claimants were sub-postmasters, many of them individuals operating as agents, so its B2B status is debatable.

## Q1. What were the exact facts, figures and contract clauses in each case?

### Takeaway

### Cited Findings

#### Uzbek Civil Code reference articles (verified text, lex.uz, Part 1)
- **Art. 9**: civil rights are exercised at the holder's discretion. Their exercise must not violate others' rights, and "добросовестность, разумность и справедливость действий участников гражданских правоотношений предполагается" (good faith, reasonableness and fairness of participants' conduct are presumed). — [lex.uz GK Part 1](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 14**: full compensation of losses unless law or contract provides for less. Losses = real damage (реальный ущерб) + lost profit that would have been received "при обычных условиях гражданского оборота" (under ordinary conditions of civil turnover). If the infringer earned income from the breach, lost profit is at least that income. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 260**: неустойка (penalty / liquidated sum) is a sum fixed by legislation or contract, payable on non-performance or improper performance. **Art. 262** sets the form of the penalty agreement. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 324**: the debtor must compensate losses caused by non-performance. Prices are taken at the place of performance on the day of voluntary satisfaction or, failing that, the day the claim is filed. Lost profit takes account of the creditor's measures and preparations. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 325**: if a неустойка is fixed, losses are recoverable only for the part not covered by it (the offsetting rule). Law or contract may instead provide for penalty only, penalty plus full losses, or the creditor's choice. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 326**: if the неустойка is "явно несоразмерна последствиям нарушения" (clearly disproportionate to the consequences of the breach), the court may reduce it. The court considers the degree of performance, the parties' property position and the creditor's interests. In exceptional cases the court may reduce it taking both parties' interests into account. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 333**: liability is fault-based unless otherwise provided. However, a person in **entrepreneurial activity** is liable unless it proves that performance became impossible due to **непреодолимая сила** (force majeure: "чрезвычайных и непредотвратимых при данных условиях обстоятельств", extraordinary and unavoidable circumstances). A prior agreement excluding liability for intentional breach is void. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 335** covers the creditor's fault. **Art. 349** covers termination of an obligation by impossibility of performance. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 383**: a material change of circumstances is a ground to amend or terminate the contract, unless the contract or its nature provides otherwise. A change is material if, had the parties foreseen it, they would not have contracted or would have contracted on significantly different terms. Absent agreement, a court may terminate (and in limited cases amend) when cumulative conditions are met, the first being that the parties assumed at contracting that no such change would occur. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)
- **Art. 384**: an agreement to amend or terminate is made **in the same form as the contract**, unless legislation, the contract or business custom indicates otherwise. A court claim for amendment requires a prior refusal or no answer within the stated period, or 30 days by default. **Art. 366** governs the form of contract. — [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181)

#### CASE 1. Cavendish Square Holding BV v Talal El Makdessi [2015] UKSC 67 (penalties; B2B share sale)
**Court and citation.** UK Supreme Court, judgment 4 November 2015. Lords Neuberger, Mance, Clarke, Sumption, Carnwath, Toulson and Hodge. Heard together with *ParkingEye v Beavis* (consumer). On appeal from [2013] EWCA Civ 1539; first instance [2012] EWHC 3582 (Comm), Burton J. — [UKSC case page](https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2013-0280); [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Facts.**
- Mr Makdessi founded what had by 2008 become the largest advertising and marketing group in the Middle East, with about 20 companies and over 30 offices in more than 15 countries. — [UKSC para 44](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- By an agreement of 28 February 2008, Makdessi and Ghossoub sold 474 shares (47.4%) in Team Y&R Holdings Hong Kong Ltd to a WPP company. Cavendish (also WPP) was substituted by novation on 29 February 2008, taking its holding to 60%. — [UKSC paras 45-46](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- The agreement was negotiated over six months. Allen & Overy acted for Cavendish and Lewis Silkin for the sellers. — [para 47](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Price structure (clause 3.1).**
- Completion Payment: US$34m.
- Second Payment: US$31.5m, paid into escrow.
- Interim Payment and Final Payment: formulae based on operating profit (OPAT), at 8× and 7–10× multiples.
- Maximum total price: US$147.5m (clause 3.3).
- The sellers warranted net asset value at about US$69.74m.
— [paras 48-49](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Key clauses.**
- **Clause 11.2** contained restrictive covenants (non-compete and non-solicit) to protect goodwill. — [para 52](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- **Clause 5.1**: a "Defaulting Shareholder" (one in breach of cl. 11.2) "shall not be entitled to receive the Interim Payment and/or the Final Payment". — [para 55](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- **Clause 5.6**: Cavendish gets a call option over the defaulter's remaining shares at the "Defaulting Shareholder Option Price", which is based on NAV and excludes goodwill. — [para 55](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- In money terms, cl. 5.1 meant Makdessi "would receive up to US$44,181,600 less". — [para 67](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- The put option (cl. 15) price was capped at US$75m per seller. — [para 50](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67); [CA judgment para 29](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2013/1539)

**Breach and evidence.**
- Makdessi admitted ongoing, unpaid involvement in the competitor Carat from February 2008. He admitted breach of fiduciary duty from 1 July 2008 and accepted that, if the covenants were valid, he was a Defaulting Shareholder. — [para 62](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- The Company's fiduciary claim settled by a payment into court of **US$500,000**. — [para 62](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- Cavendish served notice exercising the call option on 13 December 2010. — [para 59](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- The pleaded allegations were that he set up rival "Adrenalin" agencies in Lebanon and Saudi Arabia and poached clients and staff. — [paras 60-61](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Arguments.**
- Makdessi (counsel Mr Bloch QC): both clauses were penalties because they were unrelated to Cavendish's loss and were deterrent. Alternatively, the penalty rule should be extended (the Australian *Andrews* approach), or the clauses enforced only "pro tanto" (the *Jobson v Johnson* approach). — [paras 40, 84](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- Cavendish: the clauses were price-adjustment provisions (primary obligations) that protected goodwill, negotiated between equals.

**Decisions by instance.**
- First instance (Burton J): the clauses were valid ("the wolf was in the fold" once loyalty was lost). — [paras 64, 75](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- Court of Appeal (Patten, Tomlinson and Christopher Clarke LJJ): both clauses were unenforceable penalties. The court felt bound by the traditional Dunedin "genuine pre-estimate vs deterrent" framework. — [para 64](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- **UKSC**: appeal allowed and Burton J restored. Neither clause was a penalty. — [paras 78, 88](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Rule.**
- "The true test is whether the impugned provision is a secondary obligation which imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in the enforcement of the primary obligation." — [para 32](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- Clause 5.1 was in substance a price-adjustment / primary obligation. Even if the penalty rule applied, Cavendish's legitimate interest in goodwill and loyalty justified it. — [paras 75-77](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- The court rejected partial ("scaled down") enforcement of penalties and held *Jobson* wrongly decided on relief. A penalty is unenforceable, not reduced. — [para 87](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Comparative hook.** The UKSC itself noted that a corresponding rule exists in the civil codes of France (art. 1152), Germany (BGB §§343, 348, for non-commercial contracts only), Switzerland (art. 163.3), Belgium and Italy (art. 1384), in UNIDROIT Principles art. 7.4.13, and in the Council of Europe 1978 recommendation that a stipulated sum "may be reduced by the court when it is manifestly excessive". — [para 37](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**ParkingEye (contrast only, B2C).** Mr Beavis parked on 15 April 2013 and stayed 2 hours 56 minutes against a 2-hour limit. He was charged £85. The charge was not a penalty because of the legitimate interest in managing parking space. — [paras 92, 99-100](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)

**Teaching points / what to argue.**
- Debtor's side: characterise the clause as a *secondary* obligation triggered by breach. Then show its detriment is "out of all proportion" to any legitimate interest.
- Creditor's side: show the clause is a primary obligation or price term. Identify a legitimate interest beyond compensation (goodwill, loyalty, reputation, network) and stress equal bargaining power and legal advice.

**Common mistakes.**
- Applying Dunedin's four tests mechanically.
- Arguing "it's not a genuine pre-estimate, therefore a penalty". The UKSC called that an artificial distinction (para 31).
- Assuming the court will scale the sum down.

**Why debatable.** The Court of Appeal and UKSC reached opposite results on the same facts. The "legitimate interest" test is open-textured, and the line between primary and secondary obligations is manipulable by drafting (para 43 concedes this).

**Uzbek mapping.**
- FK **Art. 260** (неустойка) and **Art. 326** (court reduction when "явно несоразмерна").
- **Key contrast:** English law strikes out a penal clause entirely and refuses to reduce it (para 87). Uzbek law keeps the clause and reduces it.
- **Art. 325** offsetting contrasts with Cavendish para 76, where damages were recoverable *in addition to* the price reduction.
- *Uncertain mapping:* an Uzbek court might not treat cl. 5.1 (loss of deferred price) as неустойка at all, since it is not a "sum payable". It might instead treat it as a condition (условие) on payment, so whether Art. 326 applies is arguable. Good material for student argument.

#### CASE 2. Triple Point Technology Inc v PTT Public Co Ltd [2021] UKSC 29 (liquidated damages on termination; liability caps)
**Court and citation.** UKSC, 16 July 2021. Lord Hodge, Lady Arden, Lord Sales, Lord Leggatt and Lord Burrows. On appeal from [2019] EWCA Civ 230. First instance: Jefford J (TCC). — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf); [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)

**Facts.**
- On 8 February 2013, PTT (Thai energy company) contracted with Triple Point (a US commodities trading software developer) to design, install, maintain and license a customised commodity trading system (the "CTRM Contract"). The contract was governed by English law. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf)
- The Phase 1 contract price was US$6,920,000, paid against milestones. — [para 8](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- Stages 1–2 of Phase 1 were completed on 19 March 2014, **149 days late**, and PTT paid. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf)
- Triple Point sued on 12 February 2015 for unpaid invoices. PTT terminated on 23 March 2015 and counterclaimed. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf); [para 19](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)

**Clauses.**
- **Art. 5.3**: "If CONTRACTOR fails to deliver work within the time specified and the delay has not been introduced by PTT, CONTRACTOR shall be liable to pay the **penalty** at the rate of **0.1% (zero point one percent) of undelivered work per day of delay** from the due date for delivery up to the date PTT accepts such work…" Note that the clause is labelled "penalty" but was treated as liquidated damages. — [para 10](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- **Art. 12.3**, sentence 2 (the cap): "The total liability of CONTRACTOR to PTT under the Contract shall be limited to the Contract Price received by CONTRACTOR…" — [para 12](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- **Art. 12.3**, sentence 4 (the carve-out): "This limitation of liability shall not apply to CONTRACTOR's liability resulting from fraud, negligence, gross negligence or wilful misconduct…" — [para 12](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- The licence agreement (PLA) excluded lost profits and consequential loss. — [para 17](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)

**Decisions by instance.**
- **Jefford J**: dismissed Triple Point's claim. PTT was entitled to **US$4,497,278.40**, made up of uncapped LDs plus capped heads (cost of a replacement system and wasted costs). The capped heads were limited to **US$1,038,000**, the amount PTT had paid before termination. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf); [para 22](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- The LD component of about US$3,459,278.40 appears in secondary sources. — [secondary: search summary of law-firm notes, e.g. Fenwick Elliott](https://www.fenwickelliott.com/research-insight/newsletters/dispatch/archive/triple-point-ptt-public-company)
- **Court of Appeal** (Lewison and Floyd LJJ, Sir Rupert Jackson) made three rulings:
  - LDs accrue only for work completed and accepted before termination. Following *British Glanzstoff*, there were no LDs for work never completed.
  - All damages, including LDs, are subject to the cap.
  - "Negligence" in the carve-out means only freestanding torts, not breach of a contractual duty of care.
  - The CA figure of **US$154,662** LDs for the 149-day delay is reported **[secondary]**. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf); [para 23](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- **UKSC**:
  - Issue 1, unanimous, appeal allowed: LDs accrue until termination even where the work is never completed. After termination, general damages apply. The CA approach was "inconsistent with commercial reality". — [Press summary; paras 35, 42](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf)
  - Issue 2, 3–2 (Arden, Leggatt and Burrows; Sales and Hodge dissenting): "negligence" includes breach of a contractual duty of skill and care, so such losses are uncapped. Lord Leggatt added that, absent clear words, parties are not taken to give up valuable remedies (para 108). The dissent said this nullifies the cap for core performance (paras 125-133). — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf)
  - Issue 3, unanimous, appeal dismissed: LDs **are** within the overall cap. — [para 71](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)

**Evidence relied on.**
- The full 507-page contract was supplied at the UKSC's request, where the courts below had worked from a 74-page extract. — [para 50](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- The structure of Art. 12.3, the word "negligence" appearing twice (Arts 12.3 and 13), and the standard-form PLA provenance. — [paras 51-59](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)

**Teaching points.**
- Employer: argue that LDs are a pre-agreed simplification of loss and that accrued rights survive termination.
- Contractor: rely on the "up to the date PTT accepts such work" wording and on the cap. Argue that a carve-out covering ordinary negligence swallows the cap.

**Common mistakes.**
- Confusing whether LDs are payable with whether they are capped.
- Reading a carve-out in isolation from the whole clause.

**Why debatable.** Three levels each took a different view, and the UKSC split 3–2 on Issue 2.

**Uzbek mapping.**
- Art. 5.3 is a classic неустойка (пеня) at 0.1% per day: **Art. 260**, with possible reduction under **Art. 326**.
- The CA vs UKSC question (does a penalty run after termination, or for work never accepted?) maps onto Uzbek practice on accrual of пеня up to termination. The exact Uzbek Plenum position is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
- Liability caps map onto **Art. 14 part 1** ("если законом или договором не предусмотрено возмещение убытков в меньшем размере") and **Art. 333** (limits on excluding liability for intentional breach).
- Interplay of penalty and damages: **Art. 325**.

#### CASE 3. Hadley v Baxendale (1854) 9 Exch 341; 156 ER 145 (remoteness)
**Court and date.** Court of Exchequer, 23 February 1854. — [Wikipedia summary with citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_v_Baxendale)

**Facts.**
- Hadley and partner were millers in Gloucester. The mill's steam-engine crankshaft broke. The broken shaft had to be sent to W. Joyce & Co., Greenwich, as a pattern for a new one.
- The carriers (Pickfords, through Baxendale) delivered late, and the mill stood idle.
- The jury awarded **£50**. On appeal the court ordered a new trial, holding that lost profits were too remote because the special circumstances (the mill had no spare shaft) were not communicated.
— [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_v_Baxendale)

**Rule (two limbs).** A party is liable for losses (1) arising naturally, in the usual course of things, or (2) reasonably in the contemplation of both parties at contracting as the probable result of breach. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_v_Baxendale)

**Note.** Exact delay length and the amount claimed are not verified here. The primary text (BAILII EWHC J70) was blocked.

**Uzbek mapping.**
- FK **Art. 14** defines lost profit by "обычных условиях гражданского оборота", which loosely echoes limb 1.
- **Art. 324 part 3** requires showing the creditor's measures and preparations to earn the profit.
- *Uncertain:* the FK as read contains **no express foreseeability limit** comparable to Hadley. The CISG Art. 74 foreseeability rule would apply to international sales if Uzbekistan is a Contracting State (believed, but **[UNVERIFIED]** here).

#### CASE 4. Transfield Shipping Inc v Mercator Shipping Inc ("The Achilleas") [2008] UKHL 48 (remoteness / assumption of responsibility)
**Court.** House of Lords, 9 July 2008. Lords Hoffmann, Hope, Rodger and Walker, and Baroness Hale. Prior decisions: [2006] EWHC 3030 (Comm); [2007] EWCA Civ 901. — [Wikipedia (citing BAILII)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfield_Shipping_Inc_v_Mercator_Shipping_Inc)

**Facts [secondary].**
- Transfield time-chartered *The Achilleas* from Mercator, with redelivery due no later than midnight 2 May 2004.
- Mercator fixed a follow-on charter with Cargill at **US$39,500/day** for 4–6 months.
- The vessel was redelivered on 11 May, so the Cargill laycan was missed. Mercator had to accept a reduced rate.
- The time-charter rate of US$13,500/day (from a January 2003 fixture) appears in search summaries only. **[UNVERIFIED in primary source]**
— [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfield_Shipping_Inc_v_Mercator_Shipping_Inc); [Reed Smith note](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2008/07/house-of-lords-restricts-charterers-liability-for)

**Competing measures.**
- Owners claimed **US$1,364,584.37**: lost profit over the whole follow-on fixture.
- Charterers said damages were limited to **US$158,301.17**: the market rate minus the charter rate for the 9 overrun days.
— [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfield_Shipping_Inc_v_Mercator_Shipping_Inc)

**Decisions by instance.**
- Arbitrators, by majority: for the owners. The loss was "not unlikely" and so within limb 1. The dissenting arbitrator said the market understood liability to be limited to the overrun measure.
- Commercial Court and Court of Appeal (Rix LJ): upheld the majority award. Rix LJ called the overrun-only rule "undesirable and uncommercial".
- **House of Lords**: unanimous reversal, with the overrun measure applied. Reasoning divided:
  - Lord Hoffmann and Lord Hope: remoteness turns on what liability a party would reasonably be taken to have **assumed**, judged against market understanding. Liability for the next fixture was "completely unquantifiable".
  - Lord Rodger and Baroness Hale: decided on conventional foreseeability (the loss was not "likely" enough).
— [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfield_Shipping_Inc_v_Mercator_Shipping_Inc); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/en/perspectives/2008/07/house-of-lords-restricts-charterers-liability-for)

**Teaching points.**
- Owner: argue that foreseeability ("not unlikely") is satisfied, since every charterer knows owners fix follow-on employment.
- Charterer: argue market custom and the absence of any assumption of an unquantifiable risk.

**Common mistake.** Treating foreseeability of the *type* of loss as automatically decisive.

**Why debatable.** The Lords split on the rationale, and the "assumption of responsibility" test remains contested.

**Uzbek mapping.**
- Art. 14 (lost profit under "ordinary conditions of turnover") and Art. 324 (prices on the claim date; preparations).
- The argument "the market only expects X" could be framed in Uzbek law through **обычаи делового оборота** (business customs).
- *Uncertain:* Uzbek courts generally require proof of a causal link and actual preparations for lost profit. A direct analogue of "assumption of responsibility" is **not** found in the FK.

#### CASE 5. Tsakiroglou & Co Ltd v Noblee Thorl GmbH [1962] AC 93 (HL 1961) (frustration; Suez closure)
**Facts [secondary].**
- A CIF contract for Sudanese groundnuts, Port Sudan to Hamburg. Shipment was due in November/December 1956.
- The Suez Canal closed on 2 November 1956 and stayed closed until 9 April 1957. The only practicable route was via the Cape of Good Hope: much longer and more expensive.
- The sellers did not ship and claimed frustration.
— [e-lawresources](https://e-lawresources.co.uk/Tsakiroglou-v-Noblee-Thorl-GmbH.php); [swarb](https://swarb.co.uk/?p=148412)

**Holding.**
- There was no implied term to ship via Suez.
- Under the Sale of Goods Act 1893 s 32(2), the sellers had to ship by a reasonable and practicable route, even though it cost more.
- The Cape route did not make performance fundamentally different, so the contract was not frustrated and the sellers were liable.
— [e-lawresources](https://e-lawresources.co.uk/Tsakiroglou-v-Noblee-Thorl-GmbH.php)

**Not verified.** Tonnage, price per ton, freight increase, and the lower-court sequence (GAFTA arbitration, then Board of Appeal, then Diplock J, then CA). **[UNVERIFIED]**

**Uzbek mapping.**
- Frustration maps onto FK **Art. 349** (impossibility) and **Art. 383** (material change of circumstances / hardship).
- Tsakiroglou is the classic "increased cost is not enough" authority. This is close to Art. 383's demanding cumulative conditions.
- Art. 333 part 3 force majeure requires *impossibility*, not just expense.

#### CASE 6. Canary Wharf (BP4) T1 Ltd v European Medicines Agency [2019] EWHC 335 (Ch) (frustration; Brexit)
**Court.** High Court, Chancery Division, Marcus Smith J, February 2019. — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

**Facts.**
- The EMA (an EU agency) held an underlease dated **21 October 2014** of part of 25-30 Churchill Place, Canary Wharf.
- The term was **25 years** (1 July 2014 to 30 June 2039), with **no break clause**. — [paras 1, 92(4)](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)
- The EMA's April 2011 notification to the EU budgetary authority flagged the 25-year term with no break clause. — [para 76](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)
- The EMA received an inducement "in excess of £40 million". — [para 93](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)
- Future liabilities under the lease were approximately **£500m** (per the EMA's own evidence as quoted). — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)
- On 2 August 2017 the EMA wrote that "if and when Brexit occurs, we will be treating that event as a frustration of the Lease". The landlord (CW) sought a declaration under a CPR Part 8 expedited trial. — [paras 2-3](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

**EMA's arguments.**
1. **Supervening illegality / ultra vires**: after Brexit, paying rent would be outside its EU-law capacity.
2. **Frustration of common purpose**: the premises were meant as its permanent HQ.
3. A "self-standing" EU-law point.
— [paras 7, 34, 96](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

**CW's arguments.**
- The lease allocated the risk: it ran 25 years with no break clause.
- The alienation provisions (assignment and sub-letting) expressly contemplated the EMA leaving.
- Any frustration would be self-induced, because the EU chose the 2018 relocation Regulation.
— [paras 92(4), 201-207, 224](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

**Holding.**
- The lease was not frustrated.
- The alienation provisions "expressly cater for the complete departure of the EMA from the Premises". The EMA "assumed the risk of change over a 25-year period".
- If frustration had occurred, it would have been self-induced (para 207).
— [paras 207, 224](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

**Subsequent history.** Reports said the EMA dropped its appeal and settled later in 2019. **[UNVERIFIED]**

**Uzbek mapping.**
- **Art. 383** fits very well, especially the first condition: the parties assumed at contracting that no such change would occur. Brexit (the 2016 referendum came after the 2014 lease) is arguable either way.
- The second condition, that the risk was not borne by the affected party under the contract or business custom, is decided on the alienation clause. The exact wording of the later conditions was not fully extracted; check [lex.uz](https://lex.uz/ru/docs/111181).
- Self-induced frustration maps to Art. 383's requirement that the change arose from causes the party could not overcome.

#### CASE 7. Classic Maritime Inc v Limbungan Makmur Sdn Bhd [2019] EWCA Civ 1102 (force majeure / exceptions clause; "but for" causation; damages)
**Court.** Court of Appeal, 27 June 2019. Males LJ gave the leading judgment, with Haddon-Cave LJ among the panel. Appeal from Teare J. — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)

**Facts.**
- A long-term contract of affreightment (addendum dated **29 June 2009**) for iron ore pellets from Tubarão or Ponta Ubu (Brazil) to Port Kelang or Labuan (Malaysia). It covered seven shipments due July 2015 to June 2016. — [para 5](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- The **Fundão dam burst on 5 November 2015** stopped Samarco's production. Samarco was the sole supplier at Ponta Ubu. — [paras 6-7](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- No shipments had taken place in the second half of 2015 anyway, because demand at the Malaysian steel mills had collapsed. — [para 7](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- Vale would not have supplied from Tubarão. — [para 8](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- Teare J's findings: (1) the dam burst made the five shipments impossible; (2) **even without the dam burst the charterer would have defaulted anyway**. — [paras 9-10](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)

**Clause 32 ("EXCEPTIONS").** Neither party "shall be Responsible for loss or damage to, or failure to supply, load… the cargo resulting From: … accidents at the mine or Production facility … or any other causes beyond the … Charterers', Shippers' … Control; always provided that any such events directly affect the performance of either party under This Charter Party." — [para 11](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)

**Decisions by instance.**
- **Teare J**:
  - Liability: the charterer could not rely on cl. 32 because the "but for" test was not met.
  - Damages: only **nominal damages of US$1 per shipment**, because the dam burst would have prevented performance anyway.
  - [paras 1, 20-24](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- **CA**:
  - Cross-appeal on liability dismissed. Whether "but for" causation is required depends on construction of the clause. Here, "resulting from" plus "directly affect" required it. The *Bremer v Vanden Avenne* (GAFTA 100 cl. 21) line concerns automatic cancellation clauses and is distinguishable.
  - Shipowner's appeal on damages allowed. The obligation to supply cargo was absolute. The compensatory principle (*Robinson v Harman*; *One Step v Morris-Garner*) values the performance owed, not the reason for breach. Damages were **US$19,869,573** for the five shipments.
  - Teare J's approach "resulted in a distortion of that principle".
  - [paras 25, 81-90, 92-94](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- Any further appeal to the UKSC was not checked. **[UNVERIFIED]**

**Arguments.**
- Charterer (Mr Rainey QC): this is a force majeure clause qualifying obligations, and no "but for" proof is needed. — [paras 28-29](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- Owner (Mr Southern QC): the question is construction, and the clause requires causation. — [para 30](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)

**Uzbek mapping.**
- **Art. 333 part 3**: an entrepreneur escapes liability only by proving that performance became impossible *because of* force majeure. That is a causation requirement, close to the CA's reading.
- The finding that the charterer would have defaulted anyway is in effect a failure to prove causation under Art. 333. Plus a sanctions-style debate on lost freight under **Art. 14 / 324**.
- *Uncertain:* how an Uzbek court would value the loss (the counterfactual) when performance was in fact impossible. There is no direct FK rule.

#### CASE 8. Yam Seng Pte Ltd v International Trade Corporation Ltd [2013] EWHC 111 (QB) (implied good faith in relational contracts)
**Court.** High Court, QBD, Leggatt J (1 February 2013). — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Facts.**
- The "Manchester United Distribution Agreement" was dated **12 May 2009**. ITC (English) granted Yam Seng (Singapore) exclusive rights to distribute Manchester United-branded fragrances, mostly in duty-free outlets in the Middle East, Asia, Africa and Australasia, plus domestic sales in Hong Kong, Macau and two Chinese provinces.
- The term ran to 30 April 2010 and was later extended to 31 December 2011. — [para 1](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- The relationship soured, and in July 2010 Yam Seng terminated. — [para 2](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- The individuals behind the companies were Mr Tuli (Yam Seng) and Mr Presswell (ITC). — [paras 6-7](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Evidence.**
- Contemporaneous emails were treated as the best evidence. Presswell was found unreliable; Tuli was moderate. — [paras 8-12](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- Presswell's 23 January 2009 email said ITC had "recently signed" a Manchester United licence. That was untrue: only a 2-page "Licence Deal Summary" existed. — [paras 14-16](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- There were domestic-market undercutting complaints ("undercut by the domestic distributor by over 20%"). — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- Presswell left Tuli believing the Singapore domestic retail price had been raised to S$65, knowing that was untrue. — [para 171](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Rule.**
- English law does not imply a general duty of good faith by law into all commercial contracts.
- But a duty of good faith may be implied *in fact*, especially in "relational" long-term contracts requiring communication and cooperation such as distributorships.
- Its content includes honesty and observing standards that reasonable and honest people would regard as commercially acceptable. The test is objective.
— [paras 131-145](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Holding.**
- ITC was in breach: late shipment, failure to supply as promised, and bad faith in misleading Yam Seng about domestic pricing.
- The bad-faith breach and the threat not to honour Hong Kong/Macau exclusivity were repudiatory, so the termination was justified. — [para 230](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- Damages on the reliance (wasted expenditure) basis. The burden was on ITC to show the expenditure would not have been recouped, and ITC failed. — [paras 186-192](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- Yam Seng also succeeded under Misrepresentation Act 1967 s 2(1). — [para 230](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Figures.**
- Yam Seng's claimed net loss was **S$339,353.06**: cost of goods sold S$235,521.08, freight S$50,480.68, agents S$53,175.74, marketing S$468,115.38, less sales S$467,719.82.
- Quantum was left for the parties to agree.
- ITC's counterclaim for an unpaid invoice of **US$38,705.28** failed for circuity.
- [paras 221-229](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

**Uzbek mapping.**
- FK **Art. 9** presumes good faith ("добросовестность… предполагается"). This is the reverse of English law's default scepticism.
- In Uzbek law, good faith is a general principle, so the "relational contract" debate becomes a question of *rebutting the presumption* with evidence.
- Reliance damages map onto real damage (реальный ущерб, Art. 14).
- The misrepresentation route maps onto invalidity of transactions made under deception. The relevant FK article (believed to be Art. 122) was **[UNVERIFIED]**.

#### CASE 9. MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd v Rock Advertising Ltd [2018] UKSC 24 (no-oral-modification clauses)
**Court.** UKSC. Lord Sumption gave the lead judgment; Lord Briggs concurred on narrower grounds. — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)

**Facts.**
- On 12 August 2011 Rock took a licence of office space at Marble Arch Tower, London. The term was 12 months from 1 November 2011.
- The fee was **£3,500/month** for 3 months, then **£4,333.34/month**. — [para 2](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- **Clause 7.6**: "This Licence sets out all of the terms as agreed between MWB and Licensee. No other representations or terms shall apply or form part of this Licence. All variations to this Licence must be agreed, set out in writing and signed on behalf of both parties before they take effect." — [para 2](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- By 27 February 2012 arrears exceeded **£12,000**. The director, Mr Idehen, proposed a revised schedule by phone to credit controller Ms Evans. She denied agreeing, and her boss rejected it.
- On 30 March 2012 MWB locked Rock out, and it terminated the licence with effect from 4 May 2012. — [para 3](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)

**Decisions by instance.**
- **Judge Moloney QC** (Central London County Court):
  - Found that an oral variation was agreed and that Ms Evans had ostensible authority.
  - Found that it was supported by consideration (practical benefit).
  - But held it invalid under cl. 7.6, so MWB won. — [para 4](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- **CA** (Arden, Kitchin and McCombe LJJ): reversed. The oral agreement also dispensed with cl. 7.6, relying on party autonomy. — [para 5](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- **UKSC**: allowed MWB's appeal and restored the judge's order.
  - NOM clauses are effective, and party autonomy cuts the other way.
  - Three commercial reasons for such clauses: preventing informal undermining, avoiding disputes about terms, and policing internal authority.
  - Analogies: CISG Art. 29(2) and UNIDROIT.
  - Estoppel is the safeguard, but Rock's "minimal steps" were not enough.
  - The court declined to decide the consideration point (*Williams v Roffey* vs *Foakes v Beer*).
  - [paras 10-19](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)

**Uzbek mapping.**
- FK **Art. 384**: an amendment is made "в той же форме, что и договор". This is a *statutory* formality, so a written contract must be amended in writing even without a NOM clause. That makes the English debate largely pre-answered in Uzbek law.
- The interesting roleplay angle is the estoppel equivalent: the Art. 9 good-faith presumption, or a party's conduct accepting the new schedule. Also Art. 384's "обычаи делового оборота" exception.
- *Uncertain:* whether Uzbek courts recognise conduct-based waiver of a written-form requirement.

#### CASE 10. New Zealand Mussels case, BGH (German Federal Supreme Court) 8 March 1995, VIII ZR 159/94, CISG-online 144 (CISG Art. 35 conformity; Art. 49 avoidance)
**Source.** CISG-online 144, translation by Birgit Kurtz. — [CISG-online PDF](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)

**Facts.**
- A German fish-import buyer bought **1,750 kg of New Zealand mussels at USD 3.70/kg** from a Swiss seller.
- Delivery was in January 1992 to the buyer's cold storage. The invoice of 15 January 1992 was for **USD 6,475**, payable within 14 days.
- The veterinary agency found elevated cadmium, 0.5–1.0 mg/kg in four bags, "not yet" twice the 1990 federal public-health (ZEBS) guideline. The seller's own Swiss test showed 0.875 mg/kg.
- On 7 February 1992 the buyer sent notice by fax.
- On 3 March 1992 the buyer announced it would return the goods and complained about the packaging for the first time.
- The buyer's own laboratory found 1 mg/kg on 31 March 1992.
- The seller sued for the price plus interest. The buyer declared the contract avoided for fundamental breach.
- [paras 1-5](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)

**Expert evidence.** The federal public-health agency said ZEBS values are guidelines for preventive health protection. Occasionally exceeding them, even up to twice the value, usually causes no harm. Above twice the value, authorities usually declare the food unfit. — [para 6](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)

**Decisions by instance.**
- The Landgericht (trial court) found for the seller.
- The Oberlandesgericht (appeal court) dismissed the buyer's appeal.
- The **BGH dismissed the buyer's further appeal**, so the seller won the price.
- [paras 7-8](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)

**Rule.**
- Compliance with specialised public-law provisions of the **buyer's country** "cannot be expected" of the seller. Such standards count only if:
  1. the same standards exist in the seller's country;
  2. the buyer pointed them out and relied on the seller's expertise; or
  3. the seller knew or should have known them due to special circumstances.
- None applied. The buyer bore the burden of proof and did not allege Swiss rules. Agreeing delivery in Germany alone was insufficient. — [paras 21-24](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)
- The court left open whether Art. 35(2)(a) requires average quality or mere marketability. — [para 11](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)
- No fundamental breach under Art. 25/49(1)(a).
- The packaging complaint was untimely: avoidance on that ground was barred by Art. 49(2) because notice came only on 3 March 1992. — [para 13](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)

**Uzbek mapping.**
- If Uzbekistan is a CISG Contracting State (believed so, **[UNVERIFIED]** here), the CISG applies directly in Uzbek economic courts to international B2B sales.
- Domestically, this maps onto the FK Part 2 provisions on goods of improper quality and the buyer's notice duties. The specific article numbers are **[UNVERIFIED]**, because Part 2 of the FK was not fetched.
- The "fundamental breach" threshold for avoidance is comparable to FK **Art. 382** (termination for material breach). The exact wording of Art. 382 was not extracted.

### Inferences
- The most pedagogically valuable contrast for Uzbek students is penalties. English law is all-or-nothing: a penalty is void, and there is no reduction (Cavendish para 87). Uzbek FK Art. 326 lets the court *reduce* a disproportionate неустойка. Cavendish and Triple Point therefore teach *argument structure* (legitimate interest, proportionality) that transfers directly to an Art. 326 reduction application.
- The FK Art. 9 presumption of good faith inverts the English starting point in Yam Seng. Students should learn that in Uzbek court the burden runs the other way.
- FK Art. 384's statutory same-form rule makes MWB v Rock "easy" under Uzbek law. It is useful mainly to teach estoppel / good-faith exceptions.

## Q2. Where did the lower courts and the top court disagree, and what reasoning prevailed?

### Takeaway
In five of the verified cases the top or appellate court reversed the court below:
- **Cavendish**: CA said penalty; UKSC said valid.
- **Triple Point**: CA limited the LDs, the cap and the negligence carve-out; UKSC reversed on two of three issues.
- **MWB**: CA said the oral variation was effective; UKSC said the NOM clause was valid.
- **The Achilleas**: arbitrators and CA awarded the full follow-on loss; HL reversed.
- **Classic Maritime**: trial awarded US$1 nominal damages; CA awarded US$19.87m.

In almost every case the winning reasoning favoured **commercial certainty and the parties' written risk allocation** over a loss-based or fairness-based reading.

### Cited Findings
- **Cavendish**:
  - Burton J: valid. CA (Christopher Clarke LJ): penalties, "constrained" by Dunedin's tests. UKSC: valid under the new "legitimate interest / out of all proportion" test, which prevailed. — [UKSC paras 32, 64](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
  - The UKSC also rejected the CA's analogy to *Jobson* partial enforcement. — [para 87](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- **Triple Point**:
  - Jefford J: LDs uncapped, other heads capped.
  - CA: LDs only for completed work; everything capped; "negligence" means freestanding torts.
  - UKSC: LDs accrue to termination (unanimous); LDs are capped (unanimous, CA right); "negligence" includes contractual duty of care (3–2).
  - Prevailing reasoning: LDs exist so the employer need not prove loss, and the CA approach was "inconsistent with commercial reality". Clear words are needed to remove valuable rights (Leggatt). — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf)
- **MWB v Rock**:
  - Trial judge: oral variation invalid under cl. 7.6.
  - CA: the oral agreement dispensed with cl. 7.6 (party autonomy).
  - UKSC: the CA's "party autonomy" reasoning was "a fallacy". Autonomy includes binding oneself to a form for future variations. — [paras 4-5, 11](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- **The Achilleas**:
  - Majority arbitrators, Commercial Court and CA (Rix LJ): full loss of the follow-on fixture (US$1,364,584.37).
  - HL: overrun measure (US$158,301.17), based on market understanding / assumption of responsibility (Hoffmann, Hope) or narrow foreseeability (Rodger, Hale).
  - The dissenting arbitrator's market-practice view ultimately prevailed. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfield_Shipping_Inc_v_Mercator_Shipping_Inc)
- **Classic Maritime**:
  - Teare J and the CA *agreed* on liability: "but for" causation was required under this clause.
  - They *disagreed* on damages. Teare J awarded nominal damages; the CA awarded US$19,869,573 under the strict compensatory principle, because "the reason why a party is in breach" is irrelevant to damages. — [paras 84, 88, 90](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- **New Zealand mussels**: all three German instances agreed (seller wins). The BGH corrected the OLG on one point: from a resale perspective, exceeding twice the ZEBS value *would* raise reservations if German public law were relevant. It held German law was not relevant to conformity here. — [paras 20-21](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)
- **Canary Wharf v EMA**: first-instance decision only, so there are no appellate disagreements. — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)
- **Yam Seng**: first-instance decision only. — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)

### Inferences
- The reversals give the simulator ready-made "both sides were accepted by a real judge" material. Students arguing the losing side can cite the reasoning of a real lower court (CA in Cavendish, MWB and Triple Point; arbitrators in the Achilleas; Teare J in Classic Maritime).
- The pattern (English top courts favour textual risk allocation) differs from the Uzbek FK, which gives judges equitable tools: the Art. 326 reduction and the Art. 383 adaptation. An Uzbek judge may legitimately reach a different result on the same facts. That is good for debate.

### Gaps
- Verified lower-court reasoning in The Achilleas came only through secondary summaries.
- The detailed reasoning of Tsakiroglou's lower courts was not retrieved.

## Q3. Which cases translate best into a roleplay with two opposing counsel and a judge?

### Takeaway
The strongest roleplay candidates have a compact documentary record, a single decisive clause, and a real split of judicial opinion:
1. **Triple Point v PTT**: 0.1%/day "penalty" clause, liability cap and carve-out.
2. **Cavendish v Makdessi**: US$44m forfeiture versus goodwill.
3. **New Zealand mussels**: a USD 6,475 claim with lab reports and expert evidence. It is directly CISG, so it applies in international B2B cases.
4. **Classic Maritime**: a dam-burst force majeure clause with "but for" causation and a US$19.87m vs US$1 damages swing.
5. **MWB v Rock**: a phone call against a written clause.
6. **Yam Seng**: email trail and good faith.
7. **Canary Wharf v EMA**: Brexit hardship. It maps neatly onto FK Art. 383.

The Achilleas is excellent on remoteness but its primary facts were not verified here. Hadley and Tsakiroglou are better as short "warm-up" cases.

### Cited Findings
- **Triple Point**: evidence was the 507-page contract and its clause wording, with a clear 149-day delay and a US$6.92m price.
  - Claimant (PTT) argues accrual of the 0.1%/day sum to termination and an uncapped negligence carve-out.
  - Defendant argues "up to the date PTT accepts" and the cap.
  - The UKSC split 3–2. — [Press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0074_press_summary_f760c304b7.pdf); [Judgment paras 10-12, 50](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2021/29)
- **New Zealand mussels**: small sum (USD 6,475), dated faxes (7 February and 3 March 1992), competing lab results (0.5–1.0, 0.875, 1 mg/kg), an expert opinion from the federal health agency, and a notice-timeliness issue. — [CISG-online 144](https://cisg-online.org/files/cases/6122/translationFile/144_34291301.pdf)
- **Cavendish**: dated agreement (28 February 2008), explicit clauses 5.1/5.6/11.2, a quantified detriment (US$44,181,600), admitted breach, and opposite results at the CA and the UKSC. — [UKSC](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2015/67)
- **Classic Maritime**: the clause 32 text and two clean factual findings (impossible because of the dam; would have defaulted anyway). This is a crisp causation debate. — [EWCA](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/civ/2019/1102)
- **MWB v Rock**: a contested phone call (Idehen vs Evans) and the cl. 7.6 text. It has witness-credibility and authority (ostensible authority) issues. — [UKSC paras 2-4](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/uksc/2018/24)
- **Yam Seng**: a documentary email trail. The judge expressly preferred documents over memory, which teaches evidence technique. — [paras 8-12](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/qb/2013/111)
- **Canary Wharf v EMA**: dated letter (2 August 2017), 25-year lease with no break clause, alienation clauses, £40m+ inducement, about £500m exposure. — [EWHC](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/ch/2019/335)

### Inferences

Suggested roleplay mapping under Uzbek law:

| Case | Claimant counsel argues | Defendant counsel argues | Judge decides under |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triple Point | Recover пеня (0.1%/day) to the termination date; carve-out applies to negligent performance | Ask for Art. 326 reduction and the cap (Art. 14 part 1 allows a contractual limit) | FK Arts 260, 325, 326, 14 |
| Cavendish | Seller: forfeiture of US$44m is "явно несоразмерна" (Art. 326) | Buyer: it is a price term, not неустойка, so Art. 326 does not apply; legitimate interest in goodwill | Characterisation question; Art. 326 |
| NZ mussels | Seller claims the price (CISG Art. 53) | Buyer claims avoidance (CISG Arts 25, 35, 49) | CISG; burden of proof on the buyer |
| Classic Maritime | Owner: no force majeure causation (FK Art. 333 part 3); full lost freight (Arts 14, 324) | Charterer: dam burst = непреодолимая сила; no loss because performance was impossible anyway | Art. 333 part 3 causation and valuation |
| Canary Wharf / EMA | Tenant: material change (Art. 383) | Landlord: risk allocated by the 25-year term and alienation clauses; change was self-induced | Art. 383 conditions |
| MWB v Rock | Licensee: oral rescheduling binds; good faith (Art. 9) | Licensor: Art. 384 same form required plus cl. 7.6 | Arts 384, 366, 9 |
| Yam Seng | Distributor: bad faith and repudiation | Supplier: no breach; no causation; losses would have been incurred anyway | Art. 9 presumption; reliance losses Art. 14 |

Further inferences:
- Common student mistakes to build into scoring rubrics:
  - In penalty cases, arguing "not a genuine pre-estimate" rather than proportionality to a legitimate interest, and forgetting the Art. 325 offset.
  - In force majeure cases, proving the event but not causation ("but for").
  - In frustration / hardship cases, relying on increased cost alone (Tsakiroglou) and ignoring the contract's risk-allocation clauses (EMA alienation, no break clause).
  - In CISG cases, ignoring notice deadlines (mussels packaging complaint barred) and assuming buyer-country standards bind the seller.
- For cases with unverified secondary figures (The Achilleas, Tsakiroglou, Hadley), the simulator should either use only the verified figures listed above or label the case as "based on" the real one.

- No verified COVID-19 force majeure B2B judgment was collected. Candidates such as *FCA v Arch* are insurance cases; *Bank of New York Mellon v Cine-UK* [2021] EWHC 1013 (QB) is a lease case. Neither was checked here.
